How to Add Captions on TikTok: Check Before Posting

TikTok TranscriptSubtitle EditorPrivacy

To add captions on TikTok, record or upload your video, open the caption controls in the editing screen, generate the spoken text, and correct it before posting. Preview the whole clip with sound off, then replay it with sound to check timing and meaning. Menu labels and availability can vary with your app and account.

Start the caption checklist

Add spoken captions in the app

  1. Open TikTok and record or select your own video. Finish cuts that change the speech before reviewing captions.
  2. In the editing screen, look for Captions or the available creator caption control. Generate the text if this option is offered.
  3. Read the generated wording while listening. Correct names, numbers, missing words and punctuation before accepting it.
  4. Preview the finished clip, including its opening, transitions and ending. Check that the caption text is readable and follows the speech, then proceed to posting when satisfied.

TikTok's official introduction to auto captions describes generating captions after recording or uploading and editing their text. That announcement dates from 2021; it is background for the workflow, not a guarantee of today's exact menu positions or language list. Consult the app's current help if the option is absent. This guide does not require giving InstaScript access to your TikTok account.

Decide which kind of text you need

Spoken captions follow what people say. A short text overlay can label an object, introduce a section or add context that was never spoken. The written post description serves another purpose again: it appears with the post rather than providing a timed transcription of the recording.

If you are deciding how to add text to TikTok, write down the role first. Use a caption workflow for the dialogue, and your editor's text and timing controls for an extra label. Check both together so that a label does not cover a caption. A single opening headline leaves the remaining speech inaccessible to someone watching without sound.

An SRT file is timed text, not a finished captioned video. Keep video rendering and subtitle-file export as separate steps. This guide does not claim that TikTok's mobile posting screen accepts an SRT upload, or that InstaScript burns text into video frames.

Build a caption check card

For each short passage, check four things: the words heard, the meaning conveyed, the display interval and the part of the frame that must stay visible. Keep this card in your own working notes. It gives you a concrete reason to accept a caption or return it for correction instead of relying on how polished its font looks.

Start with the words. Listen for small terms that reverse a statement, especially negatives and exceptions. Then check quantities with their units. A number copied correctly but paired with the wrong unit still changes the instruction. When music masks a word, replay that section or consult your script; an attractive caption style cannot resolve uncertain audio.

Next compare the display interval with the action. A caption describing a second object while the first is still shown can mislead even when the sentence is correct. After trimming or rearranging clips, review those boundaries again. Earlier timing notes belong to the earlier edit, not automatically to the new cut.

Finally inspect the occupied frame. Put the text where viewers can read it without losing the demonstration, face or important object. Account for TikTok's interface in the posting preview rather than assuming a universal pixel margin. Check a small phone display as well as the larger screen on which you edited.

Worked example: a correction must survive the cut

This invented example illustrates a review decision. It is not taken from a video, a channel or a measured recognition test.

At the beginning of a clip, the speaker says, “Use the blue lid.” Immediately afterward, they correct themselves: “Actually, use the green lid for the chilled jar.” The hand moves toward the green lid while the second sentence is spoken.

A draft that keeps only “Use the blue lid” is easy to read and wrong for the completed instruction. A draft that shows both sentences together from the start reveals the correction before the speaker makes it. Neither problem is solved by changing the text color.

On your check card, record two meaning events: the initial suggestion and its correction. Match each to the appropriate part of the recording. Preserve “for the chilled jar” because it limits when the instruction applies. If the correction was cut from the video, fix the edit or the spoken explanation rather than inventing a caption that no longer matches the audio.

Now watch without sound. Can you tell that the first suggestion was revised, which lid is intended and which jar it applies to? Replay with sound afterward to confirm that the captions represent the words actually present. Keep any extra editorial clarification in a clearly separate text label.

Run the checklist before posting

Use this checklist on the final cut, after caption generation and any visual styling. Keep the original recording and a working copy until you have checked the published result.

  • Words: Verify names, numbers, units, negatives and corrections against the recording. Resolve uncertain wording rather than silently guessing.
  • Coverage: Check the first spoken word, the final sentence and every section after a cut. Look for missing passages, not just spelling mistakes.
  • Timing: Watch captions enter and leave. A correct line that disappears before its spoken phrase ends needs review in the editing workflow.
  • Readability: View the clip without sound at normal playback speed. Break up crowded text where the editor allows it, and preserve complete meaning.
  • Placement: Inspect captions with text labels and the platform interface visible. Keep important actions and objects readable too.
  • Final result: After posting, inspect the actual post. A draft preview alone does not confirm how the final video is displayed.

If captions are missing or need repair

If no caption control is available, check TikTok's current in-app help and your available editing options. Do not treat the missing control as proof that the recording contains no speech. For a manual route, add and time the spoken text in a video editor you already use, review it against the recording, and export the finished video before uploading.

For an existing post, inspect the caption-editing controls actually offered for that post. Do not assume that changing the written post description also changes timed captions. Keep a corrected master copy so that you can make a deliberate publishing decision if the needed edit is unavailable.
